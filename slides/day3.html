Session 1

Asymmetric costs

  • Compute underage and overage costs from a cost table

01
What a wrong order costs

A case short and a case left over do not cost the same

Underage: one case short

The lost margin, price minus cost, plus goodwill worth half the margin: the customer may order elsewhere next week.

Overage: one case left over

Holding at 0.4% of the cost per day for a week; for products that keep 30 days or less, 60% of the cost lost to spoilage.

Bustan's cost table is synthetic: the rates are the course's, set to look like a food wholesaler's, not quoted from one.

The goodwill share and the spoilage share are the two numbers participants will argue about; they are business judgements, which is the point. Ask the room which of the two Imad would know and which Ahlam would have to decide. The lab's stretch doubles the goodwill of the dates before Eid al-Adha.

The forecaster gives days; Imad orders weeks

Add the daily medians; add the daily variances, not the spreads

mu = q[:, 1].sum(axis=1)                                    # sum of daily medians
sd = np.sqrt((((q[:, 2] - q[:, 0]) / 2.563) ** 2).sum(axis=1))  # 0.1 to 0.9 spans 2.563 sd
order = np.ceil(np.maximum(mu + sd * stats.norm.ppf(cr), 0))
Two assumptions. Days are independent and the bands are right. Session 4 tests both on Bustan's own errors.

The normal approximation for a weekly total is reasonable for products sold most days; it is poor for intermittent items, which is why Day 3's 20 products are staples, dairy and dates. Ask the room: with yesterday's coverage of 74% for an 80% band, is this sd too small or too large? Too small.

Session 2

Optimising under capacity

  • Write an order and truck plan as variables, constraints and an objective
  • Solve it with an open solver and read the solution
  • Find which constraint binds and what relaxing it is worth
02
Why one rule per product is not enough

The 20 orders share trucks, two reefers and 200 cubic metres of depot space

Trucks

60 m3 and 20 tonnes, SAR 2,400 a trip, one supplier per truck.

Reefers

Dairy only: 40 m3 and 14 tonnes, SAR 3,200 a trip, two a week.

Depot space

200 m3 received a week at Riyadh. The newsvendor orders need 218.8.

Imad owns these numbers (labs/data/trucks.json, synthetic). The newsvendor orders of Lab 3.1 are now targets: the programme decides how far below them to go, and where, when the targets do not fit. Ask: if you had to cut 19 m3 by hand, which products would you cut?

From rule to programme

Variables, an objective and constraints, solved together by HiGHS

q[i]      = prob.add_variable(f"q_{p}", lowBound=0, cat="Integer")   # cases
trucks[s] = prob.add_variable(f"trucks_{s}", lowBound=0, cat="Integer")
prob += trip_cost * trucks + underage * short + overage * over        # objective
prob += q[i] - target[i] == over[i] - short[i]                          # distance from target
prob += lpSum(case_m3 * q[i] for i in s) <= 60 * trucks[s], f"m3_{s}"  # and kg_, reefers
prob += lpSum(case_m3 * q[i]) <= 200, "depot_space"
prob.solve(pulp.HiGHS(msg=False))

PuLP 4 attaches variables to a problem with prob.add_variable; older tutorials create LpVariable objects first and will fail with the course's version. The objective charges each case away from the target at the newsvendor's rates: a linear stand-in for expected cost, good enough to share scarce capacity. Lab 3.4's stretch solves the exact version on scenarios. Sources: PuLP on PyPI (pypi.org/project/PuLP).

Why whole numbers matter

Half a truck costs a whole trip, so trucks are integer variables

Continuous

1.26 trucks of dry goods at SAR 3,014: a price no driver charges. The plan looks cheaper than it is.

Integer

One truck or two. The solver searches the whole numbers (branch and bound) and proves the best.

1.26 is the dry goods' volume at their targets, 75 m3, divided by 60. Cases are integers too, but at hundreds of cases rounding them barely matters; at one or two trucks rounding decides the plan. HiGHS is an open-source solver for linear and mixed-integer programmes; PuLP calls it through the highspy package.

Reading the solution

A constraint binds when it has no slack; it matters when relaxing it lowers the cost

ConstraintSlackRelax it by one unit
depot_space0.02 m310 m3 saves SAR 1,703
reefers0 reefersa third reefer saves nothing: the two have 26.5 m3 free
kg_S11 kgtight because the cut was sized to fit one truck

The reefer row is the teaching point: the solution uses both reefers, so the constraint holds with equality, yet dairy's volume needs two reefers anyway. Re-solving with a relaxed limit is the honest way to price a constraint in an integer programme; the dual prices of a linear relaxation can mislead when trucks are whole numbers.

03
Scoring a forecaster by its decisions

Regret is what the order cost, minus what the best order in hindsight would have cost

Forecastquantiles for the week
→
Orderthe critical quantile
→
Demand happenscost of short and left over
→
Regretminus the hindsight cost
Takeaway. Without capacity limits the hindsight order is the demand itself, so regret is the realised cost.

Press once per step. Under the depot limit the hindsight plan would itself leave some demand unmet, so regret would be smaller than cost; Lab 3.3 leaves capacity out so that the comparison is about the forecasts alone. Regret is in riyals, which is what this morning's review asked for. Judging predictions by the decisions they cause is the idea behind decision-focused learning. Sources: Elmachtoub and Grigas, Smart "Predict, then Optimize" (arXiv 1710.08005).

Why the two scores can disagree

Error weighs every quantile and product alike; regret weighs them by what they cost

Scaled quantile loss

The 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9 quantiles of every product, each scaled to its own series.

Regret

The 0.9 quantile of rice and the 0.25 quantile of dairy, in riyals: a dairy leftover costs SAR 40 to 97 a case.

Ask the room to predict before the lab: will the two rankings of three forecasters agree over twelve weeks? Most rooms split. The lab's answer is in two parts: overall yes, week by week often not.

Session 4

Uncertainty in the plan

  • Simulate weekly demand scenarios with correlated days and products
  • Report shortage probability and fill rate of a fixed plan
  • Show how independence assumptions understate shortage risk
04
What the expected cost hides

A plan that is right on average can still run short in the weeks that matter

  • Simulate 2,000 weeks of demand from the forecast and run the fixed plan through them.
  • Report per product: the share of weeks with a shortage and the fill rate.
  • Check the assumptions on past errors: are the bands the right width, are days and products independent?

Ahlam signs plans, not forecasts. The question she asks is how often a customer will be told "not this week", and whether shortages come one at a time or together. Expected cost answers neither.

Building correlated scenarios

One shock shared by all products, one per product-week, one per day

a = np.sqrt(rho_prod)                     # all products, same week
b = np.sqrt(max(rho_day - rho_prod, 0))   # one product, all its days
c = np.sqrt(1 - a**2 - b**2)              # the rest, day by day
z = a * N(n, 1, 1) + b * N(n, 20, 1) + c * N(n, 20, 7)
daily = np.maximum(mu + scale * sd * z, 0)

N stands for standard normal draws of the given shape. The three parameters are measured on the forecaster's own errors at the five earlier origins: scale, the standard deviation of the standardised errors; rho_prod and rho_day, their correlations across products and across days. The stress case sets both correlations to 0.3, the kind of week a heatwave or an early Eid rush brings.

When the risk is too high

Optimise on the scenarios themselves: sample average approximation

Today's plan

One target per product from the forecast, then a programme that charges distance from the target.

Sample average

Shortfall and leftover variables for each of 200 scenarios; minimise the average cost under the same capacity.
