Tuned boosted trees first

  • Tune a boosted-tree model by random search under grouped cross-validation

01
What Ahlam asks

"Is the new model better?" means better than what, tuned how much?

AhlamA vendor says its pretrained model predicts late deliveries better than anything we have. Should we switch?
SarabBetter than our boosted trees after we tune them with the same effort, on our own customers. Give me today.

The target

Late: delivered after the promised hours. 18% of orders in the established regions.

The trap

An untuned baseline makes any new model look good.

The late rate is from the generated data: 18.1% in the established regions, 43.6% in the new north (Day 4). Ask the room: what is the cheapest way to make a new model win a comparison? Expected answers: tune it and not the baseline; test on data it saw; report the best of many runs. Today defends against all three.

Before any search

Write the tuning budget down before you search

Budget itemLab 1.1Why it is fixed first
Configurations20, drawn at randomMore draws always find a luckier one
Folds5, grouped by customerThe estimate the search optimises
Fits100, plus 3 refitsThe unit a pretrained model is compared in
Secondsmeasured, on a laptop CPUThe cost the adoption memo reports
A budget is part of the result. "AUC 0.78 after 103 fits" can be compared; "AUC 0.78" cannot.

In the course run the 100 search fits took about 25 seconds on a laptop CPU at 5,000 orders. Random search beats a grid at the same budget when only a few parameters matter, which is usual for boosted trees (Bergstra and Bengio). Sources: Bergstra and Bengio, Random Search for Hyper-Parameter Optimization, JMLR 13 (2012).

What the search draws from

Six parameters, each with a range that a practitioner would defend

{"learning_rate":     log-uniform 0.02 to 0.2,
 "num_leaves":        7, 15, 31 or 63,
 "min_child_samples": 10, 20, 50 or 100,
 "n_estimators":      100, 200 or 400,
 "colsample_bytree":  0.6, 0.8 or 1.0,
 "reg_lambda":        0, 1 or 5}

The best of 20 in the course run: 7 leaves, learning rate 0.021, 400 trees, 60% of features per tree. Small trees and many of them.

Ask why the best trees are so small: 5,000 orders and a target where much of the variation is the customer, which the model cannot see for a new customer. Deep trees memorise customers. The spread between the best and the worst of the 20 configurations was 0.037 AUC: that is what the budget buys.

Why the folds must be grouped

A customer seen in training is recognised, not predicted

  • A customer's distance and segment never change: together they fingerprint the customer.
  • Some customers are late more often for reasons in no column: their street, their receiving hours.
  • Random folds put the same customer on both sides; the model learns the fingerprint.
  • Bustan predicts for new and changing customers, so the folds keep each customer on one side.

In the sample

5,000 orders from fewer than 1,500 customers: most customers appear more than once.

The planted problem: every customer has a hidden effect on delay, and distance is a fixed property of the customer. Ask the room which other datasets have the same structure: patients with many visits, students with many courses, stores with many days. DS-305 covered grouped validation as a technique; here it decides which baseline counts.

The last step of the baseline

Average the top three configurations to make the baseline stable, not lucky

Why

The single best of 20 is partly the best by chance. Three good ones disagree in different places.

How

Refit each on all training orders; average the probabilities.

0.784
held-out AUC of the three-model average; the single best scored 0.785
Here the average did not raise the score. It is kept because the catalog asks for a tuned, ensembled baseline: a pretrained model must beat the strongest version.

Honest finding: in the course run the ensemble scored 0.7835 against 0.7845 for the single best, a difference well inside the noise. Ensembling buys stability across samples, which matters in Lab 1.3 where the baseline is retuned at every size. Averaging classes instead of probabilities would destroy the ranking that AUC measures.

Checkpoint

Which estimate goes in the memo?

Sarab's baseline scores 0.80 AUC under random 5-fold and 0.78 under 5-fold grouped by customer. Bustan will use the model on next quarter's orders, many from new customers. Which number goes in the memo?

0.80, because it is the model's best result
0.78, because the folds keep each customer on one side
0.79, the average of the two schemes
Either, as long as the memo names the scheme
Why. The model will meet customers it has not seen. Only grouped folds measure that; random folds let it recognise customers by their fixed fields.

Answer: B. If many choose D, ask what decision Ahlam takes from the number: she cannot correct for a scheme she does not understand. If many choose C, point out that half a leak is still a leak.

02
What a tabular foundation model does

It reads your training rows as context and predicts in one pass, with no training on them

Pretrainingonce, by the vendor, on synthetic tables
→
Contextyour labelled training rows
→
Queryyour test rows
→
Outputprobabilities, one forward pass
No parameter updates. "Fit" stores the rows; the work happens at prediction.

The TabICL paper describes in-context learning on tables as setting the training data as context for the test data and predicting in a single forward pass without parameter updates. TabPFN and TabICL are both pretrained on synthetic datasets drawn from a prior, not on real company data. Sources: Qu et al., TabICL (arXiv 2502.05564); Qu et al., TabICLv2 (arXiv 2602.11139).

Where the seconds go

For Bustan

Scoring 2,000 orders every night is cheap either way. Scoring 50,000 with 50,000 rows of context is not.

The TabICL README recommends a GPU for larger datasets. In Lab 1.2's course run the stand-in's predict step took about 0.07 seconds per fold against 0.008 for the trees: the shape, not the size, is what to notice. With the real model in Colab the gap is larger. Sources: TabICL repository README (github.com/soda-inria/tabicl).

The rule of Lab 1.2

Score both models on the same folds and pair the differences

folds = U.grouped_folds(data.customer, 5)
gbm = fold_scores(lambda: U.lgbm(**best_cfg),
                  X, y, folds)
pre = fold_scores(make_pretrained, X, y, folds)
diff = pre.auc - gbm.auc        # fold by fold
U.mean_ci(diff)

Paired

A hard fold is hard for both models; the difference cancels it.

Unpaired

Each model on its own split adds split noise to the comparison.

What changed besides the model

A pretrained model brings its own preprocessing: know what it does to your columns

Scaling

Rescaled inside the model. Trees never needed it.

Categories

The segment is stored as a code 0 to 3. A careless pipeline feeds it as a number.

Ensembling

Several passes over transformed copies of the table (TabICL's n_estimators).

Fairness cuts both ways. The pretrained model ensembles by default; that is why the baseline is ensembled too.

The TabICL README documents n_estimators as the number of ensemble members, 8 by default. This slide prepares Session 4: the vendor's claim compares an eight-member pretrained ensemble with untuned single trees. Sources: TabICL repository README.

Session 3

Learning curves decide

  • Build learning curves from repeated subsamples at six training sizes
  • Put an interval on each point and read where the curves cross
  • State where each approach wins and where the evidence is too thin to say
03
Why one comparison is not enough

A score at one training size is one point on a curve

  • Bustan has 500 orders for a new segment and 50,000 for its oldest depots.
  • Pretrained models are often strongest on small tables; tuned trees catch up as rows grow.
  • Where the curves cross, if they cross, is the decision.

Six sizes

500, 1,000, 2,000, 5,000, 10,000 orders in the lab; 50,000 supplied.

The TabICL paper reports TabPFNv2 excelling on tables with up to 10,000 samples and TabICL scaling to larger ones: published claims are about sizes, so Bustan's evidence must be too. Sources: TabICL (arXiv 2502.05564).

The design of Lab 1.3

Repeat every point on a fresh subsample to see how far it moves

Drawn orders from the pool
→
Tunethe baseline: 8 configurations, grouped folds
→
Scoreboth on the same held-out customers
→
Repeatwith a new seed
The interval is over samples. It answers "what if Bustan had drawn other orders of this size?"

Three repeats per size in the lab, five at 50,000 in the supplied file. The baseline is retuned at every size and repeat with U.tuned_ensemble, the procedure of Lab 1.1 in one call. Retuning matters: hyperparameters good at 500 rows are bad at 50,000.

Second, the tuned baseline's intervals are much wider: retuning on each sample makes it less stable. That instability is a cost too. A real pretrained model in Colab may give another picture: that is exactly why the method, not the number, is what the capstone keeps.

How to read a learning curve

A crossover counts only where the interval of the difference leaves zero

Pretrained wins

The whole interval of (pretrained minus baseline) above zero.

Baseline wins

The whole interval below zero.

Cannot tell

The interval contains zero. Say so, and say what more evidence would decide it.

Overlapping error bars are not the test. Two bars can overlap while the paired difference is clear, because the repeats are paired.

The pairing point surprises people. Each repeat draws one sample and scores both models on it, so a hard sample is hard for both. The interval of the difference is usually narrower than the two separate intervals suggest. In the course run it did not help: the differences still straddled zero.

What the curve does not say

Do not extend a curve past its last measured size

  • The supplied 50,000-order point exists because the curve could not be guessed from 10,000.
  • Run time grows differently: in the course run, 24 seconds for the baseline at 50,000 (tuning included) and 21 for the stand-in.
  • A real in-context model at 50,000 rows needs a GPU; that is a cost line, not a footnote.

Seconds are means over repeats from the course run; the 50,000-order point comes from labs/_tools/make_reference.py. Ask the room to predict how the stand-in's run time would grow at 200,000 orders and why the nearest-neighbour half of it makes the answer uncomfortable. The stretch of Lab 1.3 plots run time per size.

Three questions for any claim

Was the baseline tuned as much? Was the model scored on data it was built or selected on? How big is each win, with an interval?

The vendor and its 36 datasets are invented (data/leaderboard_claim.csv). Real benchmarks for tabular models exist and are better run than this one; the TabICLv2 paper reports results on TabArena and TALENT. The point is the reading habit, not this vendor. Sources: TabICLv2 (arXiv 2602.11139).

The first column to read

The claim's boosted trees got zero tuning trials; the pretrained model ensembled eight passes

35 / 36
wins as claimed: defaults against an ensemble
23 / 36
wins against trees tuned with 100 trials
11 / 23
wins once overlapping datasets are removed

Numbers from the course run of Lab 1.4 (data/reference/d1_4.json). Equal budget does not have to mean equal trials; it means the same compute or the same effort, stated. A pretrained model with eight ensemble members against one untuned tree model is not a fair fight in either direction.

The second column to read

A model scored on datasets it was selected on is grading its own homework

  • Pretraining overlap: the dataset, or a copy, was in the training data.
  • Selection overlap: the dataset was used to choose the model's settings or checkpoint.
  • Either inflates the score on exactly that dataset.

In the table

13 of 36 datasets carry the flag. Drop them before counting.

Pretraining on synthetic tables lowers the risk of the first kind; public validation suites keep the second alive.

Both TabICL and Chronos-2 describe pretraining on synthetic data, which reduces overlap with public benchmarks; model selection on public suites can still create it. Ask participants where they would look for the overlap list: the model card, the paper's appendix, the benchmark's own rules. Sources: TabICL (arXiv 2502.05564); Chronos-2 (arXiv 2510.15821).

The third question

Without overlap the average advantage is zero; it splits by table size

Clean datasetsCountPretrained minus tuned, AUC95% interval
All23-0.0005-0.005 to +0.004
Under 3,000 rows10+0.009+0.005 to +0.013
3,000 rows or more13-0.008-0.011 to -0.005

A bootstrap over datasets. Counting wins hid both effects.

From Lab 1.4's course run. The vendor table was generated to have a small-data advantage and a large-data disadvantage; Bustan's own curve did not show either at its sizes. That is the reply to Rafif: the claim shrinks to a small-table effect, and Bustan's own data does not confirm it.

The reply

Someone else's benchmark sets a hypothesis; your own curve tests it

The claim compared an eight-member pretrained ensemble with untuned trees. At equal tuning and without the 13 overlapping datasets, the advantage is zero on average: positive under 3,000 rows, negative above. On our late-delivery data the two approaches cannot be told apart at any size from 500 to 50,000 orders.

Three lines, as Lab 1.4 asks. Note that the reply is not "the vendor is lying": it says what the evidence supports. Day 4's adoption memo will add licence, cost and residency, and the capstone protocol makes this whole day repeatable for any task.

Checkpoint

Which fact voids the claim first?

A benchmark table shows a pretrained model winning 30 of 32 datasets. Which single fact, if true, most undermines the claim?

Why. An untuned baseline turns tuning effort into "model quality": every win may be the budget, not the model.

Answer: D. Option B is the tempting one; the problem is overlap, not publicity. Bring the room back to the three questions on the claim slide.
